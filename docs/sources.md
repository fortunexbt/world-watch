# Sources and admission

The executable inventory is [`src/collector/registry.ts`](../src/collector/registry.ts). A listed, enabled or reachable endpoint does not establish useful coverage. The deployment is paused, and this document makes no current freshness claim.

## Worldwide hazards

USGS supplies the complete past-day earthquake summary at magnitude 4.5 and above, the level at which its catalogue is complete worldwide. Each record keeps the USGS event ID and update time, magnitude, depth, review status, preferred contributing network, PAGER alert and tsunami flag. The tsunami flag marks a large oceanic event and does not mean a tsunami occurred. USGS states no country. The adapter derives one from the epicentre with the map's own Natural Earth 1:110m boundaries (`public/countries.geojson`, compiled to `src/shared/borders.json` by `scripts/borders.mjs`), never a geocoding service. A country is set only when the epicentre lies inside one polygon and at least 40 km from every edge. At that margin none of 33,806 GeoNames localities fell in the wrong country, except Simferopol, because this edition draws Crimea inside Russia. Offshore epicentres, those near a coast or border, and those in uncoded territories such as Northern Cyprus and Somaliland keep a null country. USGS-authored data is U.S. public domain, and USGS asks for credit as "U.S. Geological Survey". A preferred event ID can change when another network becomes authoritative, which can briefly duplicate an earthquake.

EMSC, the European-Mediterranean Seismological Centre, supplies a second worldwide earthquake catalogue through its [FDSN event service](https://www.seismicportal.eu/fdsn-wsevent.html). Each poll asks for the past day from the current minute at magnitude 4.5 and above, the USGS feed's floor, so both catalogues cover the same quakes, in one request of up to 500 events. The windows ending on 21 and 23 October 2026 held 19 and 27 events. A full page fails the read rather than drop older events, and a day with no event answers 204 No Content, which is a clean empty read. Each record keeps the EMSC event ID (`unid`) as its identity, the last update time and preferred origin ID as its revision, magnitude and magnitude type, depth, the Flynn-Engdahl region name as published, the event type and the solution's author. Known and suspected earthquakes are kept, a suspected one labelled as such; other event types are dropped. The country comes from the epicentre with the same map boundaries as USGS. EMSC issues no alert level, so impact stays unknown. A realtime [websocket](https://www.seismicportal.eu/realtime.html) pushes inserts and updates under the same licence, but the collector polls, so it isn't used.

The author is what makes EMSC worth adding. In a month of M4.5+ events up to 23 October 2026, EMSC computed 505 of 770 solutions itself, took 182 from NEIC, the USGS National Earthquake Information Center, and the rest from agencies such as UNA, IGC, GSRAS and GFZ. An EMSC solution of its own or another agency's corroborates a USGS record independently. One that names NEIC repeats the USGS solution and counts as one origin. So does one that names a US regional network that USGS's ComCat also prefers, because EMSC credits those under ComCat's codes (AK for Alaska, TX for Texas), and each USGS record keeps ComCat's preferred network for that comparison. A record whose author is missing leaves independence unknown. LastQuake felt reports aren't collected.

On 23 October 2026 a live read paired every one of the 22 USGS records with an EMSC record. 19 fused: 14 with EMSC's own solutions as independent origins and 5 with NEIC solutions as one origin. The other 3 stayed possible matches on separate markers, with epicentres 25 to 50 km apart at M4.8 and M4.9, beyond the 25 km floor, so the brief counts those quakes twice. 12 of the 14 independent solutions carried the USGS magnitude to one decimal, so EMSC's magnitude may come from another agency even when its location is its own; an independent origin here means an independent location and origin time. The fusion tolerances haven't been evaluated against EMSC beyond that read.

[SeismicPortal's terms](https://www.seismicportal.eu/terms.html), read on 23 October 2026, license datasets and products downloaded from its data services under CC BY 4.0, "which permits reuse, including commercial use, as long as there is attribution", with the credit "EMSC-CSEM SeismicPortal, https://www.seismicportal.eu". The website, services and databases as such stay under copyright, and mirroring or bulk-copying a database in whole or substantial part needs written permission. EMSC gives no warranty of completeness, accuracy or timeliness. An event EMSC later deletes or merges into another isn't withdrawn here; its last record stays until it falls outside the chosen time window.

GDACS supplies current Orange and Red alerts for earthquakes, tropical cyclones, floods, volcanoes, droughts and wildfires. GDACS returns at most 100 events per page. On 21 October 2026 `pagesize=200` still returned 100 and `pagenumber=2` returned the next page, and on 22 October a page past the end answered 204 No Content. The collector follows `pagenumber` until a short or empty page, for up to three pages (`max_pages`) and within the run's request budget. The Orange and Red list held 87 events on 22 October, so a normal read costs one page request and one reconciliation call, and every further 100 events costs one more request. If the page cap or the request budget stops paging early, the run fails visibly and nothing from it is published or reconciled. GDACS is slow to answer: six reads of the first page on 23 October 2026 took 42.6, 74.9, 1.6, 33.8, 23.6 and 33.0 seconds, nearly all of it before the first byte, so the collector's 15-second default failed most of them. GDACS requests therefore wait up to 90 seconds (`timeout_ms`), which covers the slowest read seen with some room and stays inside the ten-minute poll interval. Other sources keep the default.

Green alerts stay excluded. On 22 October 2026 a Green-inclusive list ran past six full pages. Its first 600 events held 331 current ones, and 329 of those were Green: 231 wildfires, 85 earthquakes, 9 floods and 4 cyclones. They would all map at impact minor, the wildfires are GWIS satellite detections beside the FIRMS thermal layer, and a first load would cost at least four page requests and about 110 ingest requests against the collector's 40-request run budget.

Only events GDACS marks as current are published, because its Orange and Red results also list archived events (62 of 63 on 22 October 2026). GDACS states no validity time, so a current event carries no expiry and stays live, however long ago it started, while GDACS lists it as current. After each complete read the collector sends the current event IDs to the source's reconcile endpoint. An incident whose event is no longer listed gets a new revision that clears `current_listing` and sets its expiry to the time World Watch saw it leave. It is not marked cancelled or corrected, and that expiry suppresses urgent alerts under the existing rules. This replaced a rule that expired events 24 hours after their latest GDACS observation: on 22 October, 68 of the 86 current earthquakes across all alert levels had a latest observation over 24 hours old, and GDACS kept some current for four days.

GDACS can mark a drought archived while its assessments continue: four of the six Orange droughts listed on 22 October 2026 were archived with an assessment dated two days earlier. Those droughts don't reach the map. Liveness depends on the collector reading GDACS, so a stopped collector leaves the last current set live. An event whose record changes while pages are being read can move to a page already read and be missed. It is ended for that poll and returns on the next, because its record has changed. An event GDACS lists again with an unchanged record stays ended until its record changes.

Each record keeps the hazard type and event ID, the episode as its revision, alert levels, start and latest dates, affected countries and the upstream hazard provider. A country is set only when GDACS lists exactly one affected country. The European Commission legal notice linked by GDACS grants CC BY 4.0 with attribution and excludes third-party material, such as the GLOFAS, JTWC and GWIS inputs named in each record. GDACS provides its data as is and not for decision making without prior confirmation, so these records are not redistributed in exports.

USGS and GDACS set impact from the native alert level and never ask a model. EMSC issues no alert level, so its records stay at unknown.

| Native level                        | Impact      |
| ----------------------------------- | ----------- |
| USGS PAGER green, GDACS Green       | minor       |
| USGS PAGER yellow, GDACS Orange     | significant |
| USGS PAGER orange or red, GDACS Red | severe      |
| None or unrecognised                | unknown     |

Alert levels are modelled estimates of losses or humanitarian impact, not observed harm. They never map to critical, which needs stated mass-casualty evidence. GDACS reports the highest alert across an event's episodes, and the current episode level stays in the record.

## United States warnings

NWS supplies its active storm-based warnings through `api.weather.gov`: tornado, severe thunderstorm, flash flood, flood, extreme wind, snow squall and dust storm warnings. NWS draws each of these as its own polygon. On 22 October 2026 the unfiltered active list held 403 alerts in 1.9 MB, and 371 had no geometry. Those are zone-based alerts (watches, advisories, statements and winter, heat, tropical and marine warnings) whose areas exist only as forecast-zone codes, and mapping them would take a further request per zone, so they are not collected. Filtering by severity instead would have kept 83 Severe and Extreme alerts, 66 of them without a polygon, and would also drop every cancellation, because NWS issues cancellations at severity Minor. The storm-based query returned 16 messages in 117 kB that day. Two were repeats of the same message, which left 14 warnings. The `status=actual` filter keeps test, exercise and system messages out, and the parser checks status again.

The API returns Atom unless asked for GeoJSON, so this source sends `Accept: application/geo+json`. The parser drops messages without an operational VTEC warning code or without a polygon. Each warning is identified by its VTEC office, phenomenon, significance and event number plus the year of its first message, because event numbers restart every January. Every NWS message about the warning is a revision of that one record, and the latest message wins when the active list repeats one. The event starts when NWS first issued the warning, taken from the earliest message the latest one references. It ends at the issuer's stated hazard end, or at the message expiry for a river flood warning with no stated end. A VTEC `CAN` or `UPG` marks the warning cancelled. An `EXP` message is not a cancellation. It leaves the warning to lapse at its stated end. Each record keeps the NWS message ID, event, VTEC string, CAP severity, certainty and urgency, issuing office, area description, times, the issuer's hazard tags (hail size, wind gust, tornado and flash-flood detection and damage threat) and the description and instruction text unchanged. The country is US, or the territory code for Puerto Rico, Guam, American Samoa, the U.S. Virgin Islands or the Northern Mariana Islands, when every UGC code in the warning names the same one.

The map point is the centre of the polygon's bounding box. The radius reaches the polygon's farthest vertex, so the circle covers the whole warned area and some ground outside it, and the excerpt says so. The polygon itself is kept as the warning's advisory area (see [issuer footprints](#issuer-footprints)).

NWS content is U.S. public domain and may be used for any lawful purpose as long as it isn't claimed as one's own, doesn't imply NOAA or NWS endorsement and isn't modified and then presented as official ([NWS disclaimer](https://www.weather.gov/disclaimer), checked 22 October 2026). The [API documentation](https://www.weather.gov/documentation/services-web-api) describes the data as open and free to use for any purpose, with a User-Agent required and an unpublished rate limit. The collector's existing User-Agent names the application but carries no contact address, which NWS recommends and does not require. Warning text is kept unchanged, and World Watch's impact labels and points are marked as its own.

| CAP severity | Impact        |
| ------------ | ------------- |
| Minor        | informational |
| Moderate     | minor         |
| Severe       | significant   |
| Extreme      | severe        |
| Unknown      | unknown       |

CAP severity states a threat to life or property, not observed harm, so each level maps one step down and Extreme never reaches critical. In the messages checked on 22 October 2026, severe thunderstorm, flash flood and flood warnings were Severe, tornado warnings were Extreme and cancellations were Minor. Tornado and flash flood emergencies don't change the mapping, because their wording states a threat rather than counted casualties. VTEC TO, SV, EW, SQ and DS warnings map to the `severe_weather` activity, and FF, FA and FL warnings map to `flood`.

## Canada warnings

ECCC supplies current public weather alerts through the MSC GeoMet OGC API at `api.weather.gc.ca`, one GeoJSON feature per alert per forecast region, each with the region's polygon. On 22 October 2026 the unfiltered collection held 53 features: 11 warnings, 30 special weather statements and 12 fog and frost advisories, 11 of them already ended. The source queries warnings only, which returned 11 features in 32 kB. Watches, advisories and statements are not collected. The query asks for up to 500 features, and a response whose `numberMatched` differs from the features returned fails visibly rather than dropping warnings. Three reads on 23 October 2026 each returned 295 warnings in 2,240,665 bytes, over the 2 MiB read cap then configured, so every ECCC read failed that day. 70% of the response is region polygons and 23% the English and French warning text, both of which the adapter needs. The API's `properties` filter works but would drop only about 1%, and it offers no geometry simplification, so the cap is 6 MiB instead: half again over a full 500-feature page at that day's size.

Each record uses ECCC's feature ID, which joins the alert and the forecast region. Two polls 20 minutes apart on 22 October 2026 caught ECCC reissuing nine features, first published at 13:25 UTC and again at 18:28 or 18:30, under unchanged IDs. A reissued warning is therefore a revision of the same record. A wind warning and a blizzard warning for the same region are separate records. The status `ended` marks the warning cancelled, since ECCC ends warnings explicitly. The event starts at ECCC's validity time and ends at its stated event end, or at the message expiry when no event end is given. Each record keeps the English and French alert names and text unchanged, the status, colour, ECCC's impact and confidence ratings, the region and province, and the issue, validity, event-end and expiry times. The country is CA. The map point is the centre of the region's bounding box and the radius reaches its farthest vertex, so the circle covers the whole region and some ground outside it. The region polygon is kept as the warning's advisory area (see [issuer footprints](#issuer-footprints)).

The [ECCC Data Services End-use Licence](https://eccc-msc.github.io/open-data/licence/readme_en/) (version 2.1.1, checked 22 October 2026) grants a worldwide, royalty-free, perpetual licence to copy, publish, distribute and adapt the information, including commercially. It requires the attribution "Data Source: Environment and Climate Change Canada" with a link to the licence where possible, forbids implying endorsement, provides the data as is, and requires weather warnings to be reproduced without altering their content or intent. Warning text is therefore kept verbatim in both languages, and World Watch's title, impact label and point are its own derivations. The [MSC Open Data usage policy](https://eccc-msc.github.io/open-data/usage-policy/readme_en/) asks users above 86,400 requests a day to get in touch and asks for a meaningful User-Agent. A five-minute poll makes 288.

| ECCC colour | Impact      |
| ----------- | ----------- |
| Yellow      | minor       |
| Orange      | significant |
| Red         | severe      |
| None        | unknown     |

ECCC's colour is its own rating of expected impact, not observed harm. Red, ECCC's "very dangerous" level, still maps to severe and never to critical. Hurricane and tropical storm warnings map to the `tropical_cyclone` activity, storm surge warnings to `flood`, and every other warning, including rainfall, wind, blizzard, heat and cold, to `severe_weather`. A rainfall warning forecasts heavy rain and does not state flooding, so it isn't mapped to `flood`.

## Warnings beyond North America

Checked on 23 October 2026. Requests used World Watch's User-Agent, and pages that render only in a browser were read in one.

No worldwide aggregator of official CAP alerts grants reuse of the alerts themselves, so each issuer's own terms decide.

- `alert-hub.wmo.int` doesn't resolve. The Alert-Hub.Org source index (`alert-hub-sources.s3.amazonaws.com/json`) lists 231 official CAP feeds and is a useful discovery list, but it has no licence field. Its aggregated feed (`cap-alerts.s3.amazonaws.com/unfiltered/rss.xml`) says "public domain", and the site says "Alert-Hub.Org asserts no copyright over the content of this website", which covers its website, not the issuers' alerts.
- The WMO Severe Weather Information Centre (`severeweather.wmo.int`) mirrors 63 issuers' CAP files. Its [notes](https://severeweather.wmo.int/note.html) say "The information is free for re-use by media or other websites", indicating that warnings are issued by the respective services, while the [WMO copyright notice](https://wmo.int/copyright) allows personal, non-commercial use "without any right to resell, redistribute". A mirror doesn't change an issuer's terms either.
- The WMO Register of Alerting Authorities lists each authority's feed URLs with no licence field.
- The IFRC Alert Hub has a GraphQL API (`alerthub-api.ifrc.org/graphql/`) and publishes no terms or licence for it. Its about page says the alerts are "solely the responsibility of the issuing agency".
- [Google Public Alerts](https://developers.google.com/public-alerts) is for publishers only. It has no feed for anyone else to read.

Many issuer feeds carry `<copyright>public domain</copyright>`, a default of the CAP feed templates. DWD's and BMKG's own terms say otherwise (CC BY 4.0 and written permission respectively), so World Watch doesn't treat that element as a licence.

Two national feeds have clear terms and are integrated.

- **PAGASA, the Philippines.** The [public alert feed](https://publicalert.pagasa.dost.gov.ph/feeds/) states: "Copyright, Philippine Atmospheric Geophysical and Astronomical Services Administration. Licensed under CC BY 4.0." No separate terms page was found. On 23 October 2026 the Atom feed listed 51 CAP messages: 23 general flood advisories, each with province polygons, and 28 tropical cyclone wind signal warnings for Typhoon Rosal (CHAMPI), each naming one or a few provinces only by PSGC code (`SAME`). The feed keeps superseded and lapsed messages for a few days, and 18 of the 51 were updates and 1 a cancellation. Three entries linked their documents through a bare IP address, `121.58.193.10`, whose TLS certificate names the host, and the same path on `publicalert.pagasa.dost.gov.ph` served identical bytes. PAGASA writes no `language` element, so CAP's default, en-US, applies.
- **INMET, Brazil.** The [warning feed](https://apiprevmet3.inmet.gov.br/avisos/rss) and the [warnings site](https://avisos.inmet.gov.br/) state: "O conteudo deste site, podera ser reproduzido desde que citada a fonte, excetuando os casos especificados em contrario e os conteudos replicados de outras fontes." Reproduction is allowed with the source cited. Modification isn't addressed, so the text stays unchanged. INMET accepts no liability for content hosted by third parties. On 23 October 2026 the RSS feed listed 80 CAP messages in Portuguese (pt-BR), each with its own polygon: 36 warnings, 1 update and 43 cancellations of warnings the feed no longer listed. The events were storms (53), low humidity (10), heavy rain (9), coastal winds (4), accumulated rain (3) and frost (1).

Both are read the same way. The registry gives each feed's URL and a pattern its document paths must match. Each poll reads the index, then each listed CAP document whose index entry is new or has a new date, four at a time. Only a link's path is used, on the index's own host, and a path that doesn't match fails the read. A document is read once for each date its index entry shows, and the checkpoint keeps what was read, so a quiet poll costs one request. A run reads documents only while its request budget leaves room to ingest them, and the rest wait for the next poll. A first read of both feeds on 23 October 2026 made 133 requests in 26 seconds and gave 48 incidents: 37 INMET warnings and 11 PAGASA flood advisory chains, 15 of them current. The public demo starts from an empty database on each run, so it reads every listed document each time, about 130 requests every ten minutes.

The CAP 1.2 reader (`src/collector/adapters/cap.ts`) is shared with BBK, whose warnings are CAP in JSON. It reads XML with or without the `cap:` namespace prefix and resolves character references in the text. For each message:

- Only `Actual` messages with `Public` scope and type `Alert`, `Update` or `Cancel` are kept. Test, exercise, system, restricted, acknowledgement and error messages are dropped.
- The info blocks in the source's language are used, or those in the first block's language when none matches. CAP lets later blocks in one language add areas but not override values, so fields come from the first block and areas from all of them. When the chosen language isn't English and the issuer also publishes English, its event, headline, description and instruction are kept beside the original as `*_en` fields. Other translations are left out. A DWD update in eight languages is the test case.
- The message identifier is its native ID. An update or cancellation is an event of the earliest message it references, and the server joins it to the incident that published any message it references. Messages from one read are ingested in the order they were sent, so PAGASA's sequence on 23 October (flood advisory #4 issued, cancelled a minute later, reissued six minutes after that) ends with the reissue live. A cancellation ends the warning it references. A cancellation that references no warning World Watch published, such as INMET's 43 for warnings it no longer lists, is stored but publishes nothing, and so does a message without geometry that references nothing published. This applies to BBK too.
- Polygons are converted from CAP's latitude-first pairs. A circle is drawn as a 64-sided polygon whose edges touch it from outside, so the area drawn and matched never falls short of it by more than 0.12% of the radius. Unclosed polygons and malformed pairs are dropped. Areas named only by geocode are listed with their codes and marked unmapped. Nothing resolves a code to a boundary, so PAGASA's wind signal warnings are stored without a map point. The point is the centre of the warned areas' bounding box, the radius reaches their farthest vertex, and the polygons are the advisory area footprint (see [issuer footprints](#issuer-footprints)), with each area's description as its label.
- The event starts at the issuer's onset, otherwise its effective time, otherwise the time sent, and ends at its expiry. Impact follows the CAP severity table under [United States warnings](#united-states-warnings), so Extreme is severe at most.
- The activity comes from the issuer's event name. Tropical cyclone, typhoon, hurricane and tropical storm, but not "hurricane force", map to `tropical_cyclone`. Flood, flash flood, storm surge, inundação, enchente, alagamento, Hochwasser and Sturmflut map to `flood`. An earthquake event in CAP category `Geo` maps to `earthquake`, and a named wildfire or bushfire maps to `wildfire`, but a fire danger rating doesn't. Any other `Met` message is `severe_weather`, so heavy rain isn't counted as flooding, and anything else, tsunami messages included, stays an official advisory, because the activity list has no tsunami entry. PAGASA's flood advisories map to `flood`, its wind signal warnings to `tropical_cyclone` and every INMET warning to `severe_weather`.
- Each record keeps the identifier, sender and sender name, message type and references, language, event, categories, severity, urgency, certainty, the sent, effective, onset and expiry times, the headline, description, instruction and web link unchanged, the area descriptions and geocodes, and the issuer's parameters. A parameter longer than 500 characters, such as INMET's list of hundreds of municipalities, is named but not kept.

Both feeds publish without a model, in the public demo and in its feeds, each record carrying its issuer's credit.

## Issuer footprints

An incident can carry the issuer's own geometry in `footprint`, beside its `location`. The location stays the marker, the spatial index key and the legacy point and radius; nothing about it changed. A footprint has a role, the URL it was read from and two simplified copies of the issuer's geometry. Each copy states a tolerance: every issuer vertex lies within that distance of it, coordinate rounding included. The detail copy starts at 0.25 km (0.33 km stated, with 0.001° rounding) and holds at most 600 positions. The display copy starts at 2 km (2.79 km stated, with 0.01° rounding) and holds at most 240. When a copy would exceed its ceiling, its tolerance doubles until it fits, up to 64 km, and a footprint that still doesn't fit is not stored. Longitudes are unwrapped so that a ring or line never jumps across the 180° meridian.

| Role                     | Source                         | Geometry                                                                                     |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `advisory_area`          | NWS, ECCC, PAGASA, INMET, AWC ash SIGMETs | The polygon the warning applies to, from the alert feature itself or its CAP areas; a SIGMET's observed and forecast ash clouds, labelled `OBS` and `FCST` |
| `inferred_affected_area` | GDACS tropical cyclones        | GDACS's modelled 60, 90 and 120 km/h wind buffers over the past and forecast track           |
| `shaking_intensity`      | USGS ShakeMap                  | Estimated shaking contour lines, one MultiLineString per MMI value; no area is inferred      |

USGS: for a quake with a PAGER alert or at magnitude 6 and above, the collector reads the event's ComCat detail GeoJSON and then the preferred ShakeMap's `download/cont_mmi.json`. These reads happen only for a record that changed since the last accepted poll, for up to four events per poll. A failed read publishes the quake without geometry and leaves it unaccepted, so the next poll reads it again; the checkpoint records reads, pending records and the last error. ShakeMap contours are MultiLineStrings (`cont_mmi.json` for us6000u43g, M7.0 Nagqu, was 44,596 bytes with nine levels from MMI 3.5 to 7.5), so they are drawn as lines and never closed into areas. ShakeMap is a USGS product and U.S. public domain like the rest of the feed.

GDACS: each event's list record links a `getgeometry` URL. For a current cyclone episode that changed, the collector reads `https://www.gdacs.org/gdacsapi/api/polygons/getgeometry?eventtype=TC&eventid=…&episodeid=…` and keeps the three cumulative buffers labelled 60, 90 and 120 km/h. Per-forecast-time buffers, track lines and uncertainty cones are left out. On 23 October 2026 CHAMPI-26's geometry was 483,875 bytes in 112 features. Earthquake geometry was also checked: it is a 100 km circle and ShakeMap-derived intensity polygons republishing the NEIC solution, which the USGS read already supplies, so GDACS earthquakes keep their centroid. Other hazard types had no current Orange or Red event to check. The [GDACS terms of use](https://www.gdacs.org/documents/2025/GDACS_Terms_of_use_Mar_25.pdf) (March 2025, read on 23 October 2026) state that GDACS products are model results from scientific and official inputs, are provided as is, may be wrong and must not override national authorities or be used for decisions without confirmation. They add no reuse condition beyond the European Commission's CC BY 4.0 notice already applied to the source. The buffers are therefore labelled as a modelled area, never as an advisory, and GDACS stays out of exports.

Watch-area alerts use an incident's footprint when it has an area: the alert fires when the watch area comes within the detail copy's tolerance of the polygon, holes included, on either side of the 180° meridian, so simplification can only widen a match. Contour lines and incidents without a footprint keep the point and radius. The browser cue applies the same rule to the display copy. A footprint also enters the alert materiality projection, so a changed polygon is a material change.

List views and SSE events carry the role and the display copy only; the detail read, the alert path and the selected incident carry the detail copy. The search index leaves footprints out (migration 0013), so coordinates never match a query. The public demo publishes display copies only. GeoJSON feeds and the GeoJSON export keep each entry's geometry as its point and add `footprints`, the display copies of its redistributable records, so GDACS buffers never leave the app. A feed carries at most 192 KiB of footprints, newest entries first, which keeps 100 entries under the 512 KiB file ceiling even at the 240-position limit; entries past the budget keep their point and the feed's `footprints_omitted` counts them. A footprint enters the alert materiality projection, so the first public run after this change dates every warning entry anew once.

## Aviation emergency squawks

ADSB.lol supplies the aircraft its volunteer receivers hear squawking 7500, 7600 or 7700 through `api.adsb.lol/v2/sqk`. The endpoint accepts a comma-separated list, so one request covers all three codes: on 22 October 2026 a `1200,7000` query returned both codes in one response. Each response carries a `total` count, and one that differs from the aircraft returned fails visibly. On 22 and 23 October the API answered with rate limits of 4 requests a second, 60 a minute and 500 a day. A five-minute poll makes 288 requests a day.

A squawk is a four-digit transponder code that the flight crew sets. 7700 declares a general emergency, 7600 a radio failure and 7500 unlawful interference. Crews also set these codes by mistake, for example while dialling another code, or as a precaution, and many 7700 flights are medical diversions or precautionary landings that end normally. Air traffic control knows whether an emergency is real, and nothing in this feed confirms one. The records therefore publish with evidence status `unverified`, never `official_advisory`. Urgent alerts need severe impact and supported evidence, so these records never raise one.

Each record is one aircraft, identified by its ICAO 24-bit address (`hex`), and one code. Its ID adds the UTC minute World Watch first saw the pair, and the collector carries that ID forward while the previous complete read still listed the aircraft with the code. Each later position is a revision of the same incident, and the start stays at the first sighting, which can trail the crew's change by up to one poll. When ADSB.lol stops listing the aircraft with the code, the collector's complete current list no longer contains it and the incident ends as GDACS events do: `current_listing` is cleared and the expiry records when World Watch saw it go, without marking it cancelled. That happens when the crew clears the code, and also when the aircraft lands and switches off or leaves receiver coverage, which the feed can't tell apart. A later sighting opens a new episode, and an aircraft that changes from 7700 to 7600 ends one episode and opens another.

The point is the position in the response, or readsb's `lastPosition` when the live position is more than 60 seconds old. Its accuracy is `point`, and the radius covers the ground the aircraft could have covered between that fix and the response at its reported ground speed, with a floor of 1 km for multilateration error. The aircraft keeps moving between polls, and the record states the time of the fix. The country is derived from the point and the map's own boundaries, as for USGS epicentres, and stays empty over the sea or near a border. An aircraft listed without any position is stored so its episode continues, but it isn't mapped.

Each record keeps the address, callsign, code and its meaning, readsb's ADS-B emergency status, aircraft type designator, ground flag, barometric altitude, ground speed, track, vertical rate, the military database flag, the position source (ADS-B, multilateration or TIS-B), the position time and the first and latest sightings. It leaves out the registration and any owner or operator field. Aircraft flagged with a Privacy ICAO Address or on the FAA LADD list are skipped, because their operators asked not to be shown publicly. A private aircraft's callsign can still be its registration.

| Code | Meaning               | Impact        |
| ---- | --------------------- | ------------- |
| 7500 | unlawful interference | significant   |
| 7700 | general emergency     | minor         |
| 7600 | radio failure         | informational |

The impact is a cautious reading of a self-reported code, not a report of harm. A genuine 7500 would be grave, but the code alone is unconfirmed and often set in error, so it stays at significant unless an issuer confirms it, and no issuer feed is integrated. 7700 declares an abnormal situation and says nothing about harm. 7600 starts a lost-communications procedure.

The [API documentation](https://api.adsb.lol/docs) (checked 22 October 2026) states that the API "as well as all data ADSB.lol makes public" is licensed under the [Open Database License 1.0](https://opendatacommons.org/licenses/odbl/1-0/). It says the API is free to use, that "in the future, you will require an API key which you can get by feeding to adsb.lol", and asks production users to get in touch first. The [docs page](https://www.adsb.lol/docs/open-data/api/) marks the public API deprecated in favour of re-api, which only feeders can reach from their station's address. The [privacy and licence page](https://www.adsb.lol/docs/privacy-license) provides the data as is, asks users to indemnify ADSB.lol against third-party claims arising from non-conforming use, and gives `info [at] adsb.lol` as the contact. A resumed hosted deployment should contact ADSB.lol first and expect to need a feeder key.

The ODbL permits storage, display and redistribution with these conditions:

- A publicly used produced work, such as the map, needs a notice that it contains information from the database under the ODbL (section 4.3). The map's attribution line and each record's attribution carry that notice with links to ADSB.lol and the licence, and the record text repeats it.
- The extracted records form a derivative database that must stay under the ODbL when publicly used (4.4). Exports, which already carry each record's attribution and licence basis, therefore include them, and they mustn't be relicensed. The rest of World Watch's database is a collective database the ODbL doesn't reach (4.5a), since aviation records aren't merged with records from other sources.
- Whoever receives the derivative database or a produced work must be offered the derivative database or the method that made it (4.6). The export is the first and this adapter, in a public repository, is the second. Sign-in is allowed if it doesn't restrict those rights (4.7c).

Coverage depends on where volunteers run receivers. It is dense over Europe and North America and thin or absent over the oceans, polar routes and much of Africa and Asia. An emergency outside coverage never appears, and an empty list doesn't mean no aircraft is squawking an emergency code. On the evening of 22 October 2026 the list was empty at 23:37 UTC, while the same endpoint returned 1,044 aircraft squawking 1200.

## Aviation hazard advisories

### Volcanic ash SIGMETs

The NOAA Aviation Weather Center (AWC) serves current SIGMETs from meteorological watch offices worldwide through its [data API](https://aviationweather.gov/data/api/). On 23 October 2026 the API's OpenAPI file marked the international endpoint `/api/data/isigmet` deprecated in favour of `/api/data/sigmet`, which adds US domestic SIGMETs, and the documentation set these rules: at most 100 requests a minute, at most 400 entries from most endpoints, a custom User-Agent, no CORS, and 204 No Content for a valid query with nothing to return, except in GeoJSON. At 11:05 UTC the unfiltered list held 131 SIGMETs: 53 thunderstorm, 42 turbulence, 15 icing, 11 volcanic ash, 5 mountain wave, 3 tropical cyclone and 2 convective. The source asks for volcanic ash only (`hazard=va`), 8 to 15 kB a read that day. Thunderstorm, turbulence and icing SIGMETs are routine flight hazards that would crowd the feed, as routine notices crowded Europe Watch's, and tropical cyclone SIGMETs repeat storms GDACS already maps. The JSON format is read rather than GeoJSON because only JSON carries AWC's receipt time and the issuer's issue time. A list of 400 entries fails visibly, since it could be truncated.

AWC lists a SIGMET's observed and forecast ash clouds as two entries with the same message, for example series `2` and `2F` for Fuego. The adapter joins them into one record per SIGMET, identified by the issuing office, the series the message itself states and the start of validity, because series numbers restart every day: `PAWU-KILO3-20260927T2145`. A replacement SIGMET for the same volcano has a new series number and becomes a new record, so a volcano can show two overlapping SIGMETs, as Whakaari did with NZKL 8 and NZKL 10 on 23 October. The event starts and ends at the SIGMET's own validity, and its publication time is when AWC received it. The issuer's header can be wrong: the Fuego SIGMET of 23 October was headed 12:30 UTC but reached AWC at 06:32. Each record keeps the message unchanged, the office, FIR, series, volcano as the message names it, issue and receipt times, validity, base and top in feet with a band such as `SFC/FL380`, movement and change. A base written without the FL prefix, such as `110/FL380`, is left unparsed by AWC and the band stays empty.

An office ends a SIGMET early with a `CNL SIGMET` message. None appeared in AWC's decoded list in 33 raw snapshots from 16 to 23 October or 49 half-hourly JSON snapshots on 22 and 23 October, and the list for the current time matched the live list exactly, so the adapter can't mark a SIGMET cancelled. One cancelled early stays on the map until its stated end, and the record says so.

The map point is the centre of the bounding box around all of a SIGMET's clouds, and the radius reaches their farthest vertex, as for NWS polygons. AWC writes longitudes past 180° for areas across the antimeridian, up to 187° for the Sheveluch forecast cloud on 27 September, and these are wrapped first. The clouds themselves are kept as the SIGMET's advisory area, each labelled `OBS` or `FCST` as AWC labels it (see [issuer footprints](#issuer-footprints)). A SIGMET drawn only as a line has no area and is left out; none of the ash SIGMETs checked was. The country comes from the area's centre and the map's own borders, as for USGS epicentres, so an ash area over the sea or near a border has none.

Every ash SIGMET maps to impact minor. It warns aircraft of an ash cloud in a stated block of airspace, states no ash concentration and reports no harm, and ICAO's format gives volcanic ash no severity levels. The eruption is a separate GDACS volcano record with its own alert level. The two are not linked yet.

AWC is part of the National Weather Service. The [NWS disclaimer](https://www.weather.gov/disclaimer) (checked 23 October 2026) puts NWS information in the public domain "unless specifically noted otherwise", and adds that "Third-party information and imagery are used under license by the individual third-party provider" and "Please contact the third-party provider for information on your rights to further use these data/products." SIGMETs from the NWS's own watch offices, KKCI (AWC), PAWU (Alaska) and PHFO (Honolulu), are NWS products. All 11 ash SIGMETs of 23 October came from other states' offices: NZKL (New Zealand), MHTG (Central America), WIII and WAAA (Indonesia), SKBO (Colombia) and SEQU (Ecuador). In daily samples from 24 September to 22 October, 2 of 362 ash SIGMET entries came from a US office, PAWU, for Sheveluch ash drifting into the Anchorage FIR. ICAO Annex 3 has states issue SIGMETs and exchange them over aeronautical channels, which is why AWC holds them, but grants no public reuse. The [WMO Unified Data Policy](https://wmo.int/wmo-unified-data-policy-resolution-res1) commits WMO members to exchange core data free and unrestricted, but neither that page nor the [policy FAQ](https://wmo.int/sites/default/files/2023-09/2021-MISC-I-FAQ-Data-Policy-21851_en_rev_14-6.pdf) (both read 23 October 2026) says that aeronautical products such as SIGMETs are core data, and the resolution's own text didn't load (`library.wmo.int` answered 503). With no grant found for foreign offices' SIGMETs, the source shows them in the signed-in view, sets `redistribute: false` and records an unresolved permission, which keeps it out of the public demo and its feeds. The US-issued SIGMETs are public domain and could be published as a separate source, but at about one entry in 180 it isn't worth one yet. The test fixture holds only PAWU's SIGMETs for the same reason.

### Conflict Zone Information Bulletins

The European Union Aviation Safety Agency lists its [Conflict Zone Information Bulletins](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/domains/air-operations/czibs) with JSON, CSV and RSS exports. On 23 October 2026 the list held 34 bulletins back to 2016, 16 active and 18 withdrawn. The JSON export (14 kB) has each bulletin's node ID, subject, status, issue date with EASA's UTC offset, validity date, affected countries and last update, but no text. The CSV export (104 kB) has the CZIB number, affected airspace and countries, applicability, description, recommendations and referenced publications, but no node ID or update time. The RSS feed has titles and links only. The source reads the JSON and CSV exports once an hour and joins them by subject and issue date, which matched all 34. A bulletin missing from either export, or a CSV header that changes, fails the read rather than publishing a bulletin without its text.

EASA's node ID stays the same across revisions. Syria's bulletin, first issued on 31 March 2017, is node 20599 at its 20th revision, CZIB-2017-03R20, so each revision updates one record. EASA writes the number in several forms (`CZIB-2026-09-R1`, `2026-03-R14`, `2025-02 R2`, `CZIB-2017-03R20`), so it is kept as written. A withdrawn bulletin is marked cancelled, because EASA withdraws bulletins explicitly. The event starts at the issue date and is published at the page's last update. It ends at the close of the validity date in UTC, because EASA states a date without a time ("16/11/2026, unless reviewed earlier."), and a bulletin without one has no end until it is withdrawn. Each record keeps EASA's fields unchanged.

The affected countries, as EASA names them, are matched to the gazetteer's country names, and the circle covers the largest landmass of each on the map's Natural Earth borders, as for routing drops. A bulletin for one country has country accuracy and that country. One for several, such as the Persian Gulf and Gulf of Oman, is an area with no single country. Bahrain is too small for the 1:110m borders, so it adds a square around its gazetteer centre and radius. Many bulletins cover only part of a country, such as Russia west of 60° East, Pakistan's Baluchistan and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa provinces or Mali below FL260, and the circle overstates those; EASA's affected airspace stays in the record. No footprint is drawn: EASA describes airspace by FIR, flight level and route in text, and a country outline would look more precise than the bulletin is. Bulletins that name no country (Venezuela and neighbouring airspace, Iran and neighbouring airspace, Kenya) and one for "Democratic People's Republic of Korea", which the gazetteer doesn't list under that name, are kept without a map point. All of them are withdrawn.

Every bulletin maps to impact significant. EASA issues a CZIB to share information and recommendations about risk to civil aviation from a conflict zone, and most recommend not operating in part of the airspace. It is a risk assessment for air operators, not a report of harm, so it never reaches severe or critical. The structured brief counts bulletins in force and names the countries they cover.

The [EASA copyright notice](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/copyright-disclaimer) (checked 23 October 2026) says "Reproduction is authorised, provided the source is acknowledged, save where otherwise stated", and the bulletin pages state nothing otherwise. It also says website material is "Not necessarily comprehensive, complete, accurate or up to date". The records keep the text unchanged and carry the acknowledgement, set `redistribute: true` and join the public demo and its feeds.
## GNSS interference grid

World Watch builds its own daily map of where aircraft lost satellite navigation accuracy, from ADSB.lol's openly licensed flight history. It is a context layer in the private app, like the thermal and Copernicus layers, and never an incident. It is shaded by the share of aircraft reporting degraded GNSS accuracy and integrity. That can indicate jamming or spoofing, and it is also caused by faulty equipment, manoeuvres that block the antenna and gaps in receiver coverage, so a shaded cell does not prove interference.

### The archive

ADSB.lol publishes each UTC day of `/var/globe_history` from its readsb containers as GitHub releases of [`adsblol/globe_history_2026`](https://github.com/adsblol/globe_history_2026), to which the older `adsblol/globe_history` name redirects. On 23 October 2026 the GitHub API reported the repository's licence as `ODbL-1.0`, and its README says the database "is made available under the Open Database License". The release `v2026.10.22-planes-readsb-prod-0`, published at 03:26:51 UTC the next day, is one tar archive split into three parts of 2,000,000,000, 2,000,000,000 and 61,022,208 bytes (4.06 GB). It holds 78,974 gzipped readsb `trace_full` JSON files, one per aircraft address (3.05 GB compressed, 19.73 GB inflated, 112,028,683 position points), 49 replay heatmap files (0.95 GB) that the grid doesn't read, ACAS advisories, and a README stating the day, the release and the ODbL grant. The README also records that feeders waive their rights to their own data under CC0, which doesn't change the ODbL on the database. A `staging` release of the same day exists, and the README says to use whichever of prod or staging isn't much smaller. The `mlatonly` releases carry only multilateration positions, which have no accuracy fields.

The [readsb trace format](https://github.com/wiedehopf/readsb/blob/dev/README-json.md#trace-jsons) stores each point as an array of time offset, latitude, longitude, altitude or `"ground"`, ground speed, track, flags, vertical rate, an optional details object and the position source. About one point in four carries the details object, which holds the aircraft's own NACp, NIC and SIL among its other fields.

### Method

Each aircraft's trace is read once. A details report counts only if the position came from the aircraft's own ADS-B (`adsb_icao`), not multilateration, TIS-B or ADS-R, and the aircraft was airborne at more than 50 knots, the speed readsb's own lost-GPS check uses. Each counted report is classed by three fields:

| Class    | Rule                                     | Meaning                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| -------- | ---------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| healthy  | NACp ≥ 8, NIC ≥ 7 and SIL 3              | The minimum [14 CFR 91.227(c)(1)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-91/subpart-C/section-91.227) requires of ADS-B Out: position accuracy under 0.05 NM, containment radius under 0.2 NM, integrity 1e-7 per hour |
| degraded | NACp ≤ 1 and NIC ≤ 6                     | Estimated position uncertainty of 4 NM or more, or unknown, with integrity below the rule                                                                                                                                                      |
| other    | anything else with all three fields      | Places the aircraft in the cell without counting it as degraded                                                                                                                                                                               |

An aircraft counts only if it reported healthy at least once that UTC day, so avionics that never report good accuracy are left out instead of reading as interference. In each cell it then counts once, as degraded if any of its reports there was degraded. Of the day's 59,804 aircraft with classified reports, 2,541 never reported healthy and were left out. Requiring both fields to agree matters: across the day, 8,603 airborne reports had NACp ≤ 1 with NIC ≥ 7, a contradictory pair, while over the Baltic 4,631 of 4,709 reports with NACp ≤ 1 also had NIC 0.

The method follows [GPSJam](https://gpsjam.org/faq), which publishes its formula and bands but not code or data, and reimplements it independently. GPSJam's FAQ gives `percent_bad_aircraft = 100 * (num_bad_aircraft - 1) / (num_good_aircraft + num_bad_aircraft)`, colouring a cell green under 2%, yellow from 2 to 10% and red over 10%. Its author described "good" and "bad" as reporting under 1 nautical mile of uncertainty and then over 4 ([Hacker News, 27 July 2022](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=32247823)), and readsb's own check uses the same marks, NACp ≥ 4 and NACp ≤ 2 ([`track.c`](https://github.com/wiedehopf/readsb/blob/dev/track.c)). World Watch keeps GPSJam's discount of one degraded aircraft and its bands, and differs in three ways. The healthy reference is the stricter FAA minimum rather than 1 NM, the degraded class also needs NIC, and the healthy report may come after the degraded one, because a whole day's trace is available at once. GPSJam counts an aircraft as bad only if it reported good accuracy first, which misses a flight that departs inside interference.

Cells are 0.5° of latitude by 0.5° of longitude, aligned on 180°W, so a cell never straddles the antimeridian and 180°E falls in the 180°W column. At 45°N a cell is 55.6 by 39.3 km, 2,185 km², close to the H3 resolution 4 hexagons GPSJam uses (1,770 km² on average) where most receivers are, and a cruising aircraft crosses one in about four minutes, long enough for several accuracy reports. Interference reaches aircraft over hundreds of kilometres, so finer cells would mostly add noise. A fixed grid also lets each worker count into flat typed arrays of 259,200 cells with no hashing. Cells with fewer than 10 counted aircraft are left out, so a blank cell means no data rather than no interference, as on GPSJam.

### The derived grid

`npm run gnss:grid -- --out <dir> <part files...>` reads the parts in order as one stream, or stdin when no file is given, so `curl -sL <part URLs> | npm run gnss:grid -- --out <dir>` builds the grid without the archive touching disk. It never extracts files or holds the archive in memory. The main thread walks the tar headers and copies each aircraft's gzipped trace into 4 MB batches, and worker threads inflate and scan them into their own counters, which are summed at the end. The scanner jumps between details objects with native `indexOf` and reads only those points' fields, without building objects. It reads exactly what `JSON.parse` reads, which a test checks on randomised traces, and on the whole day the scanner and a `JSON.parse` baseline wrote byte-identical grids. The run refuses an archive whose README doesn't name the day and the ODbL.

It writes `gnss-<date>.json`, with one `[south, west, aircraft, degraded]` row per published cell, and `manifest.json` with the date, cell size, thresholds, counts, source release, licence, ODbL notice and the grid's size and SHA-256. For 22 October 2026 the grid held 15,824 of the 22,843 cells any counted aircraft reached, 257,951 bytes (64,012 gzipped). 3,260 cells had at least one degraded aircraft, 631 fell in the middle band and 216 in the top band. The densest top-band cells were over Bahrain and the Persian Gulf (134 of 625 aircraft in one cell), around Ankara, the Romanian and Bulgarian Black Sea coast, Moscow, Gdańsk and eastern Türkiye, where jamming is widely reported, and also over São Paulo and Mexico City, which this data can't explain.

On an Apple M4 shared with other jobs, from local files with a warm page cache, one day took:

| Run                                    | Wall time | CPU time | Peak RSS | Archive read | Position points | Accuracy reports |
| -------------------------------------- | --------- | -------- | -------- | ------------ | --------------- | ---------------- |
| `JSON.parse` per trace, one thread     | 77.0 s    | 83.2 s   | 450 MB   | 53 MB/s      | 1.45 M/s        | 0.36 M/s         |
| byte scanner, one thread               | 66.7 s    | 68.0 s   | 394 MB   | 61 MB/s      | 1.68 M/s        | 0.42 M/s         |
| `JSON.parse`, eight workers            | 20.0 s    | 132.4 s  | 1,008 MB | 203 MB/s     | 5.6 M/s         | 1.40 M/s         |
| byte scanner, four workers (default)   | 22.6 s    | 82.5 s   | 394 MB   | 180 MB/s     | 4.96 M/s        | 1.24 M/s         |
| byte scanner, eight workers            | 13.2 s    | 94.7 s   | 620 MB   | 308 MB/s     | 8.5 M/s         | 2.12 M/s         |

Timings move with the machine's other load: three four-worker runs took 20.6, 22.6 and 32.0 seconds, and two eight-worker runs 13.2 and 14.6. Piping the parts through stdin wrote the same grid, byte for byte, as reading the files.

Inflating the traces costs about a third of the CPU time and is the floor. On the same 3,000 traces the first scanner, which called native `indexOf` for every string, was slower than `JSON.parse` (88 to 105 MB/s of inflated JSON against 124 to 150 MB/s), because each call costs more than a short loop. Plain loops for short searches raised it to between 481 and 493 MB/s against 405 to 420 MB/s over four repeats. Reading tar members straight into the batch buffer, which removed a copy and an allocation per aircraft, and a 64 MB heap ceiling per worker together cut eight workers' peak from 973 MB to 620 MB at the same wall time. Four workers are the default because they keep the peak near a single thread's while running three times as fast; `--workers 8` is the fastest measured.

### Serving it

The private app reads `gnss/manifest.json` and the grid it names from its own build, so a run writes into `dist/gnss` after `npm run build`, which empties `dist`. The Worker serves every non-API path to the owner only. Turning on "GNSS interference from aircraft reports" under Map layers reads both files once, checks that the manifest names the ODbL and matches the grid, and draws the cells under the incident markers with a legend for the three bands. A missing grid shows a notice instead. Selecting a cell shows its aircraft and degraded counts and share. The panel and the map's attribution carry the ODbL notice with links to the release and the licence, and the panel links the grid and manifest for download. The coverage view lists the grid with its build time and marks it stale two days after it was built.

### Rights

The ODbL permits this with conditions, and the grid is a derivative database of ADSB.lol's.

- Showing the map publicly is a produced work that needs a notice "reasonably calculated" to make viewers aware the content came from the database under the ODbL (section 4.3). The map's attribution and the panel carry the licence's own example notice, with hyperlinks.
- The grid is a derivative database, and a publicly used derivative database, or one behind a publicly used produced work, must be offered under the ODbL only (4.4a and 4.4c). The manifest states the ODbL and the notice, and the grid is never mixed with other sources' records.
- Whoever receives a publicly used derivative database or produced work must be offered the entire derivative database or the method, free of charge over the internet (4.6). The panel links the grid itself, and this repository holds the method.
- Use inside an organisation is not public use and doesn't trigger share-alike (4.5c), so the owner-only app meets the licence already; it shows the notice anyway.

The layer is compiled out of the public demo. Before it goes public, the demo would need to publish each day's `gnss-<date>.json` and `manifest.json` as downloadable files under the ODbL beside the map, keep the notice next to the layer and in the attribution, keep the grid out of any combined file under other terms, and say how the files were made, which this section does. ADSB.lol's API documentation asks production users to get in touch first, and its [privacy and licence page](https://www.adsb.lol/docs/privacy-license) asks users to indemnify it against claims from non-conforming use, so the owner should contact ADSB.lol before publishing, as for squawks. The grid holds counts only, with no aircraft address, callsign or track, so LADD and privacy-address aircraft are not identifiable from it, though they are counted.

### Limits

- Coverage is ADSB.lol's volunteer receivers, and only aircraft that fly with ADS-B appear. Conflict zones that civil aviation avoids, most oceans and much of Africa and Asia stay blank.
- Spoofing that keeps reporting good accuracy is invisible here. A spoofed aircraft only shows when its avionics notice and report degraded accuracy.
- One run covers one UTC day. Interference for an hour can shade a cell for the whole day, and nothing runs on a schedule while the project is paused.
- No labelled set of interference events exists to measure how often a shaded cell is real interference. Clusters of shaded cells shouldn't become incidents without one.

## Internet routing drops

World Watch detects national drops in Internet routing itself, from the RIPE NCC's RIPEstat [`country-resource-stats`](https://stat.ripe.net/docs/data-api/api-endpoints/country-resource-stats/) endpoint. For each country RIPEstat counts the IPv4 prefixes and origin networks (ASNs) that the five RIRs' delegation files register to it and that RIPE RIS route collectors see in global routing. The adapter asks for 14 days of these counts at hourly resolution. RIPEstat answers one country per request and refuses a list, so the source reads three of the map's 175 countries per five-minute poll in a fixed rotation and keeps its position and every open drop in its checkpoint. Each country is read about every 4 hours 55 minutes, which is 864 requests a day, under the 1,000 a day above which RIPEstat asks users to register their `sourceapp`. On 22 October 2026 a response was at most 90 kB and took under a second, and an hour's counts appeared about two hours after it ended. With the second low hour needed to confirm a drop and up to five hours until its country's turn, a drop reaches the map about four to nine hours after it starts.

Each hour is compared with the median of the normal hours in the seven days before it, and at least 24 are needed. An hour is low when half the baseline's prefixes are gone, or a quarter of them together with a tenth of its networks. Aggregating routes lowers a prefix count without losing networks, so a smaller prefix loss on its own doesn't count. A drop starts at the first of two consecutive low hours and ends at the first of two consecutive recovered hours. Low hours never enter a baseline, so a long drop can't become its own reference, and missing hours are skipped rather than read as low. Countries whose baseline has fewer than 50 visible prefixes are not evaluated, which in the 30 days to 22 October 2026 left out 13 of the 175. Data more than six hours old neither opens nor ends a drop.

Each drop is identified by its country and first low hour, such as `IR-2019-11-16T20`, and the event starts at that hour. A drop still low at the latest hour is listed as current with no expiry, as current GDACS events are. Once two recovered hours are measured, the next read revises it with the first recovered hour as its expiry. A drop that disappears without a recovered hour, because its start is more than seven days before the latest hour or because RIPEstat revised its counts, is ended by reconciliation at the time World Watch noticed and is not marked cancelled. A stopped collector leaves open drops live. The point is the centre of the country's largest polygon on the map's Natural Earth borders, with a radius to its farthest vertex, so the circle marks the country rather than an affected area. Each record keeps the baseline window, its median counts, the lowest counts, the largest drop as a percentage and the first low, last low and recovered hours, and links to the RIPEstat query that covers them.

| Largest share of prefixes lost | Impact      |
| ------------------------------ | ----------- |
| 25 to 49%                      | minor       |
| 50 to 89%                      | significant |
| 90% or more                    | severe      |

A routing loss measures reachability, not harm, so it never maps to critical. A drop is a measured change in routing, not a confirmed shutdown, and it states no cause. A government order, a power cut, a cable cut, a large network's failure and a routing change look alike here, and a shutdown that filters traffic while its routes stay announced doesn't show at all. Prefixes count by registration country, not where they are used.

Replayed at six-hour steps over RIPEstat's history, the detector found 10 of 14 well-documented national events: Egypt on 27 January 2011 (97% of prefixes lost), Syria on 29 November 2012 (100%), Gabon on 7 January 2019 (73%), Venezuela's blackout from 8 March 2019 (four drops of 28 to 40%), Iran on 16 November 2019 (53%), Ethiopia on 30 June 2020 (100% for five hours), Myanmar on 1 February 2021 (51%), Sudan on 25 October 2021 (69%), eight of Iraq's exam shutdowns in June 2023 (47 to 73%) and the Iberian blackout on 28 April 2025 (33% in Spain). It found nothing for Kazakhstan in January 2022 (11% at most), Syria's exam shutdowns in June 2023, Cuba's grid collapse in October 2024 or Iran's shutdown in June 2025, whose routing barely moved. Across all 175 countries from 6 September to 22 October 2026 it raised three drops: Guyana on 23 September (68%), Niger on 24 September (37%) and Burundi on 15 October (49%). IODA recorded BGP outages in all three countries at the same hours. IODA recorded 576 BGP country events in that period, so this detector reports only large national drops and misses smaller ones by design. No labelled set exists to measure its precision and recall properly.

The [RIPEstat Service Terms and Conditions](https://www.ripe.net/about-us/legal/ripestat-service-terms-and-conditions/) (checked 23 October 2026) provide the service for network analysis, monitoring, debugging and research (3.1), forbid re-packaging, compiling or re-distributing RIPEstat Data (3.3), allow no commercial use without written permission and let data-specific terms prevail (4.3). The [RIS Commercial Use Terms and Conditions](https://www.ripe.net/analyse/internet-measurements/routing-information-service-ris/commercial-use/) grant a revocable default permission to provide reports or other derivatives based on RIS data to third parties. The conditions are to accept the RIPE NCC's [Terms of Service](https://www.ripe.net/about-us/legal/terms-of-service/), show the RIPE NCC logo, link to `https://www.ripe.net/ris` and include RIS's boilerplate description. World Watch publishes its own drop detections with their baseline and lowest counts rather than the series, carries the link and boilerplate in every record's attribution and leaves the records out of exports. The [logo page](https://www.ripe.net/about-us/press-centre/ripe-ncc-logos/) asks to be contacted at comms@ripe.net before the main logo is used on a website, so no logo is shown yet. Before a public deployment shows these records, the owner should agree the logo with the RIPE NCC and register the `sourceapp` with stat@ripe.net, as the [Data API usage rules](https://stat.ripe.net/docs/data-api/ripestat-data-api/) ask of regular users.

## Worldwide news coverage signals

GDELT's three ways in were checked on 22 October 2026.

- The [DOC 2.0 API](https://blog.gdeltproject.org/gdelt-doc-2-0-api-debuts/) answered an `artlist` query for protest coverage with titles, URLs, domains, languages and publisher countries, but no location for what an article describes. A `timelinevolraw` query for one place and theme answered HTTP 429 with "Please limit requests to one every 5 seconds" and a pointer away from the API for high-traffic users. Ranking hundreds of places every 15 minutes that way would break the limit.
- The [GEO 2.0 API](https://blog.gdeltproject.org/gdelt-geo-2-0-api-debuts/) answered 404 to every query form at 23:37 UTC, although [GDELT's data page](https://www.gdeltproject.org/data.html) still lists it.
- The [GDELT 2.0 Global Knowledge Graph files](https://blog.gdeltproject.org/gdelt-2-0-our-global-world-in-realtime/) are published every 15 minutes in an English stream ([`lastupdate.txt`](https://data.gdeltproject.org/gdeltv2/lastupdate.txt)) and a stream machine-translated from 65 languages ([`lastupdate-translation.txt`](https://data.gdeltproject.org/gdeltv2/lastupdate-translation.txt)). Each record carries the article URL, its headline, GDELT's themes and geocoded places with character offsets, and a tone score. English files held 1,224 to 1,705 records in 5.0 to 7.4 MB of ZIP that day, and the 14:00 UTC translated file held 3,600 records in 14 MB. The two collector sources read these files.

The [GDELT terms of use](https://www.gdeltproject.org/about.html#termsofuse) make every GDELT dataset available "for unlimited and unrestricted use for any academic, commercial, or governmental use of any kind without fee" and allow redistribution, as long as any use or redistribution cites the GDELT Project with a link to https://www.gdeltproject.org/. The map, feed, detail panel and brief all carry that citation. The terms cover GDELT's own data, not the linked articles. World Watch therefore keeps only each article's URL, headline, domain, GDELT language code, tone, matched themes and file time, plus counts by place and hour, for eight days. It never fetches an article, and it drops GDELT's quotations, emotion scores, names and image links. Headlines belong to their publishers, so the sources grant no model use and no redistribution, and a signal never enters the analysis queue. Queueing one for the worker would first need publisher rights for that article.

A record becomes a mention when one of the matched GKG themes appears within 150 characters of a place GDELT geocoded to a city or landmark (GKG location types 3 and 4). Country and province mentions have no useful map point and are skipped. The themes are `PROTEST` and demonstrator tags for protests, `STRIKE` and striker tags for strikes, `ARMEDCONFLICT`, `REBELLION`, `INSURGENCY` and `SEIGE` for armed conflict, `TERROR` and the bomb and explosive weapon tags for attacks and explosions, the coup-plotter actor tags for coups, `REFUGEES`, `DISPLACED`, the CrisisLex displaced-or-evacuated tag and refugee camps for displacement, `EVACUATION`, `CURFEW` and `STATE_OF_EMERGENCY`, and `NATURAL_DISASTER*` and `MANMADE_DISASTER` for disasters. Broad themes such as `KILL`, `SECURITY_SERVICES` and `MANMADE_DISASTER_IMPLIED` tag too much ordinary coverage and are left out. An article counts for at most its three nearest places. In the 14:45 UTC English file, 120 of 1,705 records matched across 106 places, and in the 14:00 UTC translated file 243 of 3,600 matched across 182 places.

Mentions are clustered by GDELT's place ID, so 50 articles about one protest in one city become one signal with its article count, distinct domains, distinct headlines and top sources. Signals are counted and ranked in distinct headlines, compared without the outlet name a syndicated copy appends, so one wire story carried by 60 outlets counts once. In a replay of 16 hours of files on 23 October, counting articles instead put one Belfast politics story, syndicated by 60 sites, in three of the top four places. A signal's window is the current UTC hour and the five before it. Its baseline comes from the same place over the seven days before the window, stream by stream: each stream's headlines per ingested file, times that stream's files in the window, gives the headlines the window would hold at the usual rate. A collection gap lowers both sides alike, and an outage in one stream neither hides nor fakes a change in the other. The ratio is window headlines against that expectation, each plus one, so a place GDELT never placed before still gets a finite ratio. A signal is marked rising when the ratio is at least 3 with at least 3 domains and 5 distinct headlines, and it ranks by its excess over the expectation in Poisson standard deviations. Until every stream with files in the window has 12 hours of earlier files there is no baseline, no ratio and nothing marked rising, and places rank by distinct headlines instead. A place needs two domains in the window to be listed at all. Replaying 26 hours of live files through this code on 23 October (205 files, 363,196 records, 25,638 matched) listed 100 places and marked 11 rising, led by rescue coverage of the Tibet earthquake in Sichuan, a Vrancea earthquake in Romania, a West Bank shooting near Hebron and prison riots in Quito. Taipei, with heavy but steady typhoon coverage, was listed and not rising. The structured brief names at most two rising signals, for example "News coverage of protests in Lima, Peru rose sharply in the last 6 hours."

Each source reads its listing with an ETag and ingests at most two files a run from the eight newest listed, two hours' worth, newest first, so a fresh collector backfills two hours within half an hour. GDELT can list a file before it can be fetched. On the night of 22 October the translated files from 23:00 UTC still answered 404 more than an hour after they were listed, and on the morning of 23 October the translated stream ran about 45 minutes behind its listing. A file that 404s doesn't count towards the two, stays eligible until it drops out of the newest eight, and is tried again on the next run. A run in which no pending file can be fetched fails visibly and backs off. The server records each file once, so a retried upload doesn't count twice. A file is rejected unless it is a single deflated ZIP entry that decompresses within 192 MB.

These are GDELT's machine readings of the news, and they go wrong in known ways. On 22 October GDELT placed an article about the Tibet earthquake in Beijing, tagged a music prize story as armed conflict, and placed government news in Washington, Moscow and the Kremlin, where the capital stood for its government. The English stream leans towards US outlets. Translation can lose or invent a theme. GDELT names countries with FIPS codes, so the country comes from the map's own boundaries, as for USGS, and is left empty near a coast or border. A signal says coverage of a place changed, not that anything happened there, and it isn't a displacement or crowd measurement.

## Satellite imagery context

Checked on 23 October 2026. Both services are read by the viewer's browser, only when asked, with no key, account or World Watch server in between.

The map can lay a NASA daily mosaic over the basemap's land and water and under its roads, borders and labels: NOAA-20 VIIRS true colour (`VIIRS_NOAA20_CorrectedReflectance_TrueColor`), Terra MODIS true colour (`MODIS_Terra_CorrectedReflectance_TrueColor`) or NOAA-20 VIIRS night lights (`VIIRS_NOAA20_DayNightBand_At_Sensor_Radiance`). Tiles come from GIBS's Web Mercator WMTS, `https://gibs.earthdata.nasa.gov/wmts/epsg3857/best/{layer}/default/{day}/{matrix}/{z}/{y}/{x}`. The [EPSG:3857 capabilities](https://gibs.earthdata.nasa.gov/wmts/epsg3857/best/wmts.cgi?SERVICE=WMTS&REQUEST=GetCapabilities) put both true-colour layers on `GoogleMapsCompatible_Level9` and night lights on `GoogleMapsCompatible_Level8`. A tile past the last level answers 400, so the source stops there and the map enlarges it. Tiles and the per-layer DescribeDomains day lists (`.../best/1.0.0/{layer}/default/{matrix}/all/{from}.xml`, about 360 bytes) answer with `access-control-allow-origin: *`. At 11:17 UTC the VIIRS day list already ended on 23 October, a mosaic that fills in pass by pass, so the default day is the newest finished one and the caption marks a current day as still filling in. A day the list leaves out, such as 22 and 23 February 2018 for NOAA-20, answers 404 and the control says NASA lists no imagery for it. Bands and resolutions come from [Worldview's layer descriptions](https://github.com/nasa-gibs/worldview/tree/main/config/default/common/config/metadata/layers), and overpass times from each orbit: NOAA-20 ascends about 13:30 and descends about 01:30 local solar time, and Terra descends about 10:30. A daily mosaic has no cloud-cover figure, and the caption says so.

[GIBS's data use guidance](https://nasa-gibs.github.io/gibs-api-docs/#data-use-guidance-and-acknowledgements) says "NASA promotes full and open sharing of data" and asks clients to acknowledge "the use of imagery provided by services from NASA's Global Imagery Browse Services (GIBS), part of NASA's Earth Science Data and Information System (ESDIS)". The [Earthdata data use guidance](https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/engage/open-data-services-software-policies/data-use-guidance) licenses NASA mission data without a marked restriction as CC0 and says the material "may not be used to suggest or imply endorsement". The imagery control carries the acknowledgement with "no NASA endorsement", and the map credit names GIBS, the sensor and the day.

An incident's detail panel can list the Sentinel-2 L2A and Sentinel-1 GRD scenes over a box about a kilometre wide around its mapped point, from ten days before its event time to ten days after or now. The browser asks the Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem's [STAC catalogue](https://documentation.dataspace.copernicus.eu/APIs/STAC.html), `https://stac.dataspace.copernicus.eu/v1/search`, which needs no key and answers with `access-control-allow-origin: *`. The `fields` extension cuts a reply to about 2 kB per collection, against about 70 kB per item without it. Each row gives the platform, acquisition and catalogue times in UTC and the distance from the event time. Sentinel-2 rows give `eo:cloud_cover`, which covers the whole 110 km tile rather than the point, as the caption says. Sentinel-1 rows give mode, polarisations and orbit direction. Each opens the [Copernicus Browser](https://browser.dataspace.copernicus.eu/) anonymously on that UTC day at the point, in true colour for Sentinel-2 or in the scene's radar mode for Sentinel-1. The browser accepted the dataset IDs `S2_L2A_CDAS`, `S1_CDAS_IW_VVVH`, `S1_CDAS_IW_HHHV`, `S1_CDAS_EW_HHHV`, `S1_CDAS_IW_VV` and `S1_CDAS_IW_HH`, and fell back to Sentinel-2 with "Selected dataset does not exist!" for an unknown one. For a point in Manila and an event time of 18 October 00:00 UTC, the catalogue listed five Sentinel-2 scenes and two Sentinel-1 scenes between 8 and 23 October, including a Sentinel-2B pass two hours after the event time.

The [CDSE terms and conditions](https://dataspace.copernicus.eu/terms-and-conditions) make Sentinel data free, full and open and say its use "shall be governed by" the [Sentinel data legal notice](https://sentinels.copernicus.eu/documents/247904/690755/Sentinel_Data_Legal_Notice). The notice allows reproduction, distribution and communication to the public with the notice "Copernicus Sentinel data [Year]". The terms keep the portal's other content "for non-commercial use", with no right to redistribute it, and section 9 forbids bypassing quotas by "creating multiple user accounts". The words "for individual use" aren't in the terms. They're on the [About page](https://dataspace.copernicus.eu/about): "The data will be available free of charge via designated quotes for individual use. Users that wish to build large scale operations can use practically unlimited resources available under commercial terms." The [quotas page](https://documentation.dataspace.copernicus.eu/Quotas.html) sets those quotas per user account. Its column for S3, OData and STAC has no monthly request quota, and its 2,000 requests a minute "Only applies to S3". The wording therefore describes the account quotas for downloads, Sentinel Hub processing units and openEO credits. It doesn't restrict anonymous catalogue searches, which each viewer's browser makes for itself, two per button press. It would apply to quicklooks rendered through one World Watch Sentinel Hub account for every public viewer, and World Watch renders none.

Every STAC item also has a `thumbnail` asset, a 343 px JPEG of about 7 kB. Its URL on `datahub.creodias.eu` redirected to `zipper.creodias.eu`, which served it without a token as an `application/octet-stream` attachment with a session limit of 1,000. The legal notice would allow showing Sentinel data, but the CDSE terms say "To access Earth observation data at the Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem, individuals and entities need to register as a user", and no CDSE document grants embedding these files from a CREODIAS host without an account. That isn't a clear permission, so World Watch never requests a thumbnail and leaves previews to the Copernicus Browser.

Both features are in the public demo. The imagery is openly licensed with the acknowledgement shown, the scene list carries catalogue fields with the Sentinel notice, and neither needs a key, an account or a server. Requests carry no credentials or referrer. GIBS still sees the area and day being viewed, and Copernicus sees the incident's point and time window. With Save-Data or `prefers-reduced-data`, the map draws the 256 px tiles at 512 px, a quarter of the requests at half the detail.

The app shell sets no Content-Security-Policy. Only the sign-in page has one, and it loads no imagery. A policy for the app would need `connect-src https://gibs.earthdata.nasa.gov https://stac.dataspace.copernicus.eu` for these features, because MapLibre fetches raster tiles. A test pins every URL the imagery module builds to those hosts and the Copernicus Browser.

## Admission contract

Before enabling a source, establish its geographic scope, publisher and upstream origin, update semantics, typical latency, reliability, terms, attribution, and separate permissions for access, storage, model use, display and redistribution. Record the specific grant or blocker. Public reachability is not a grant.

Use approved HTTPS origins and bounded response sizes. Do not follow report-supplied URLs automatically or forward application/service credentials to publishers. Conditional requests and checkpoints advance after durable acceptance. Repeated content, boilerplate and unchanged revisions should not create model work.

Use native episode identity where available. Cross-source bundles need evidence of shared event identity and must preserve contradictions and original source links. Reposted material from one origin counts as one reporting lineage. New material facts, corrections and cancellation can require a new analysis; "one call per event" cannot mean freezing the event forever.

Missing geography remains unknown. Municipality centroids are approximate areas, not exact incident coordinates. Planned assemblies, crowd estimates, observed attendance, evacuation orders and measured displacement are distinct observations. Dataset silence does not establish that nothing happened.

Retention and deletion must propagate through source text, derived claims, search, caches, exports and local spools. Dataset permission and personal-data risk must be reviewed before publishing examples. This repository intentionally omits the collected operational corpus.
